iTAB — a specialist consultation service

iTAB is a health and wellness store on the Russian market: supplements, lab discounts, expert content. I designed a mobile consultation service for the same ecosystem. Specialists get a dashboard, a schedule and an application form that lists them in the catalogue. Clients find, book and pay without leaving the store.

The mobile version is postponed: it is still to be built. Its logic became the web service. That one is live, it has its first metrics behind it, and I designed it too.

Mobile app and web

My Role
Product designer. Owned the end-to-end specialist and client flows, from onboarding and scheduling to booking and payment.

Problem

iTAB sold supplements, but the people who actually recommend supplements — nutritionists, therapists, preventive-medicine doctors — lived outside the app. A client who wanted advice left for a messenger or a clinic, and the recommendation came back as a screenshot at best. The store had no way to host a consultation, and a specialist had no reason to bring clients into it.

Task

Design a consultation service inside the existing app: a way for specialists to get listed and paid, a way for clients to find, book and pay for a consultation, and the operational layer in between — schedules, confirmations, cancellations, chat. It had to work as one account with two contexts, not a second app.

Users

Two roles with opposite needs. A specialist wants clients and as little paperwork as possible, but the platform can only pay them once their legal status and qualifications check out. A client wants to trust a stranger with a health question, so credentials, price and the next free slot come before anything else. I worked both roles out with the PM.

Solution

I designed a new consultation section from scratch within the existing account. Specialists apply and manage their schedules and consultations there; clients find a specialist, book and pay without leaving iTAB.

1. Getting a specialist into the catalogue

The entry point is the application. It opens with the reasons to fill it in: recommendations for clients, a steady stream of clients, a payout every month. And it promises fifteen minutes. The form is cut into short steps with a progress bar. Closing it offers to keep the progress.

The order of the steps is the platform’s: without a registered employment status it cannot pay a specialist, so the application asks for that before the catalogue ever shows them. The result goes to moderation and comes back within a working day.

2. One account, two contexts

A specialist is also a customer of the store, so the dashboard is a second context of the same account, not a second login. Before the application it holds one call to action and the referral code; after moderation it gains consultations, the schedule and recommendations, and a single button switches back to the client profile with its orders and appointments.

The dashboard also holds the partner programme. Specialists bring clients into the store through a personal promo code: the client gets a discount, the specialist gets cashback on every order and a bonus once the month crosses a threshold. The code sits in the dashboard next to consultations. That closes the loop: advice, a recommendation with a code, a purchase in the same app.

3. Finding and booking a specialist

On the client side the service inherits the language of the store: a strip of specialties, sorting and filters for format, price and experience. The specialist card puts credentials, patient types, the next free slots and the price above the fold.

Booking is date, time and format. Then a checkout with a comment for the doctor, the offer agreement and the cancellation rule. Payment closes with a sheet that leads to the client’s consultations. The store home carries the new section next to the products.

4. Two clocks, one appointment

iTAB sells across a country eleven time zones wide, so a consultation has two clocks: the client’s and the specialist’s. Every screen states which one it is showing. The client picks a slot in their own zone and the list says so. The specialist builds the schedule in theirs, under a line that says clients see their own time. Checkout is where the two meet, so it prints both — 17:00 for the client, 10:00 in Moscow for the doctor.

A client can move between booking and appointment. The entry keeps the time it was booked in and shows the time in the client’s current zone.

5. Running consultations

For the specialist the service is a schedule and a list. The schedule is built in two moves: pick the dates open for booking, then the slots for each day. A repeat option covers a regular week. The list separates planned consultations from completed ones. A planned one can be confirmed, taken to chat or cancelled with a reason the client receives.

Chat sits next to the booking. Moving a consultation is a message, not a phone call outside the app.

Alongside the main flow I designed the states a service lands in during its first weeks. All are built from the existing components and content. The three riskiest: an application returned for fixes, a date with no free time, a slot already booked that cannot simply be removed.

6. The web version

Result

The work defined a connected consultation service inside the iTAB ecosystem. Specialists move from application and moderation to managing availability and consultations in one account; clients discover, compare, book and pay without leaving the store. The same model became the foundation for the web service that shipped. Usage numbers for it stay under iTAB’s NDA, so this case shows the model rather than the metrics.

An extension of the store, not a second product: the service reuses the existing account, catalogue language and checkout instead of introducing another login and a disconnected experience.
